A revenue-first operating plan for Foreguild: an AI job board where forecasts pick who does each job before the work starts, and Seer markets on revenue decide how the guild grows and spends.

1Summary

Open bounties pay one winner and throw away everyone else's work. Once AI agents can enter, entries multiply and the prize money per entry shrinks. Foreguild sells the opposite: one job, one assigned agent, paid only when the work passes tests the buyer agreed to up front, with an optional refund guarantee. Forecasters compete to predict which agent will deliver, and they are paid only when they beat the guild's reputation model. Bigger choices (prices, new verticals, budgets) go to Seer conditional markets on revenue net of serving costs and of what each proposal spends.

2The problem, measured

I pulled all 2,091 listings from Superteam Earn's public API, a large crypto bounty board, and kept the 485 bounties from 2026 that paid in stablecoins and received entries. Superteam labels each listing HUMAN_ONLY or AGENT_ALLOWED, which gives a rare public comparison of open competitions with and without AI agents.

  1. Open competition wastes most of the work. A median agent-allowed bounty draws 115 entries for a few prizes. Prize money per entry is a median of $5. This is not any one agent's expected pay, which depends on skill. But it does mean most of the work done goes unpaid, and the sponsor has to review all of it.
  2. The buyers exist and pay. Sponsors paid about $727k into Superteam stablecoin bounties from 1 January to 8 November 2026, leaving out the $1M hackathon, or about $71k a month. They have budgets, recurring needs and crypto wallets. Foreguild does not need to create demand. It needs to give these buyers a better deal.
  3. Automated payments are numerous but small. x402 has processed over 100M payments, but the total value is only tens of millions of dollars, and most single payments are under 10 cents. So the base case relies on human-led teams paying for outcomes. AI buyers are an extra channel.

This contest shows the same pattern: more than 20 public plans appeared on GitHub within a week, competing for about $5.1k. Foreguild's offer for Seer's next brief is a shortlist of three agents, payment only for the one chosen (or for all three in a creative shortlist), and delivery in days.

Caveat. Agent-allowed bounties lean toward content tasks, which draw more entries anyway, so not all of the 3× gap is caused by letting agents in. The direction matches drdz23's study of 394 bounties on another board. The method and raw counts are in section 12.

3What Foreguild sells

Assured Outcomes. A buyer describes a job. Foreguild turns it into a brief: a fixed price, a due date and written acceptance tests. One agent is assigned, and that agent is paid only when the work passes. If the buyer added assurance and the work fails, the buyer chooses a refund or a rework.

Subjective creative work comes later as Shortlist Contests. Three assigned agents each make one option, a Seer opportunity market plus the buyer picks one, and all three agents are paid. It's the same mechanism as Seer's own rebrand contest, but with about three paid entries instead of 100 or more unpaid ones.

Why buyers switch from bounties

  • One checked result instead of 100 to read. Reviewing entries is the sponsor's hidden cost, and the guild does it before delivery.
  • Pay on acceptance, with a refund if assured. A bounty pays its winner even when no entry is good.
  • Days, not weeks, and the brief stays private when it needs to.

Growth is driven by buyers, not an assumed curve

Monthly work = active buyers × spend per buyer. Assumptions:

  • Retention: 80% of active buyers buy again the next month.

5How a job flows

Assignment (step 3): forecasts that must beat reputation

Seer's documentation describes AI markets: several candidates are proposed, forecasters trade on which will succeed, the best is used and the rest are cancelled. Foreguild uses the same idea to assign work, with three safeguards so the forecasts are honest and never cost more than they are worth.

  1. Shortlist. The router picks up to three bonded agents. The guild's reputation model gives each one a baseline chance of being accepted on first delivery, based on audited history in that vertical.
  2. Forecasts. Forecasters (agents or humans, each with a small bond) give P(accepted | agent i). The operator of a shortlisted agent may not forecast that brief.
  3. Selection with exploration. 90% of the time the job goes to the agent with the best combined forecast, adjusted for delivery time. 10% of the time it goes to a random shortlisted agent, with known probabilities πi. A decision rule that never explores lets forecasters steer which agent gets scored, so a scoring rule cannot be proper without it (Chen & Kash, "Decision markets with good incentives"). Exploration also gives new agents a path in.
  4. Scoring. Only the assigned agent j's outcome is ever observed. A forecaster's score is (1/πj) × [log score of their forecast − log score of the reputation baseline]. Positive scores share a pool capped at 1% of the price. Negative scores are deducted from the forecaster's bond, up to twice what they could have won. Any unspent part of the pool stays with the guild.
  5. Large briefs ($2,000 and up) get a real Seer market: a categorical parent for which agent is assigned, and one conditional child per agent for "accepted on first delivery?". The guild adds 1% of the price as liquidity. Only the assigned agent's child resolves.

What this costs if forecasts add nothing: at most 1% of the price, and in practice close to zero, because only improvements over the baseline are paid. With no forecasts, routing falls back to the reputation model. Payment is linear in the log-score improvement while the cap does not bind, which keeps truthful reporting optimal in the normal range. The cap and the bond-limited losses weaken that guarantee at the extremes, so caps are set well above typical scores, and whether forecasts beat the baseline is tested in public (falsifier 3). Forecasts should help most where reputation is thin: new agents, new verticals, unusual briefs.

Assessment and cheating incentives (steps 5–8)

  • Acceptance tests are fixed when the buyer funds escrow. A rejection must name a failed test.
  • The verifier comes from a different model family than the worker, which cuts down on errors they share. It doesn't remove them, which is why humans also audit.
  • Audit sampling (Deep Funding-style "distilled human judgment"). Human reviewers score a random sample of accepted jobs, plus every job where the verifier and the buyer disagreed. A verifiable random seed picks the sample. The sample rate depends on the agent's tier.
  • Portable track record. Audited results go to the agent's ERC-8004 reputation registry. This record is technical, not legal: the operator also signs an agreement at registration and is the legal party.

For a proven agent, one bad job carries a small direct penalty. The main deterrent is losing a tier that took 20+ jobs to earn. A proven agent earning $250 per job across a pipeline of briefs loses far more from demotion than any single bad delivery can gain. Agents whose verifier and buyer disagree are sampled at 100%.

One brief, end to end (illustrative)

Buyer: a Solana protocol that last quarter ran a $500 "write a thread about our feature" bounty. Bounties like that drew 145–587 entries on Superteam in 2026. Brief: a technical explainer (an X thread and a docs page) on one feature. Price $300, due in 72 hours. Acceptance tests:

  1. Eight factual claims, each linked to docs or a commit.
  2. Every number matches a named on-chain dashboard on the delivery date.
  3. No claims about price or returns.
  4. Required tags and a disclosure that the content is paid.

Assignment: three shortlisted agents with reputation baselines of 0.80, 0.80 and 0.70. The forecast pool says 0.86, 0.74 and 0.71, because one forecaster knows the second agent's operator has no Solana docs experience. Agent 1 is assigned (greedy draw). Delivery: 41 hours. The verifier (a different model family) confirms all eight citations and catches one stale TVL figure, which is fixed before delivery. The buyer accepts in 20 hours. Not sampled for audit (the agent is proven).

Ledger: the buyer pays $300 plus $18 for assurance. The agent receives $255 ($25.50 held back for 14 days). The guild keeps a $45 fee plus the $18 premium; its costs are verifier ≈ $0.5, intake ≈ $0.3 and forecaster pool $1.10 (of a $3 cap; paid because the forecast beat the baseline on the observed agent).

Against the bounty: one paid entry instead of hundreds, delivery in 2.6 days instead of a week or more, and nothing for the sponsor to sort. This is a constructed example with plausible figures, not a real transaction. The first real ones will be published on the public dashboard (section 10).

6Futarchy: three loops

Hanson's rule is "vote on values, bet on beliefs." Every decision goes to the cheapest loop that can make it well.

The metric: Guild Net Revenue

GNR(W) = outcome fees plus assurance premiums on briefs whose due date falls in window W, minus fees and premiums refunded on those briefs, minus their assurance claims. All figures are taken as of a fixed cutoff, the question's opening time. Claims still open at the cutoff count at the published expected claim rate. Later corrections only change the dashboard, never a settled market.

GNC(W), the decision metric, = GNR(W) − 5.5% × GMV(W) − S. The 5.5% is the published variable-cost rate; S is the proposal's declared incremental spend (0 in the NO branch). The market seed is spent whichever branch wins, so it is not part of S.

  • Due date, not acceptance time. The due date is fixed when the buyer funds escrow, so neither buyers nor agents can move revenue across a window edge by delaying acceptance.
  • "Refunds" means fees and premiums returned. Escrow principal returned to a buyer was never revenue.
  • Exclusions: related parties (agent operators, guild signers, market liquidity addresses, and wallets within two funding hops of them; anyone can challenge the list at Kleros). Then each buyer cluster is capped: its counted contribution = min(cᵢ, 0.15 × Σ cⱼ), where the sum is the uncapped total after exclusions. A cluster with history also has anything above max(2 × its trailing 90-day monthly average, $1,500) excluded. New clusters are limited only by the 15% cap.
  • The model in section 4 shows economic revenue. The decision metric applies these exclusions on top, so it is the same or lower.
  • Retainers count pro rata by day of service.

The definition is pinned by IPFS hash in every market question. A reference script is pinned by commit hash. If the two disagree, the written definition wins.

Decisions are judged net of their own cost

Revenue alone would approve "spend $4,000 to gain $600." So the markets forecast GNC: revenue minus the variable cost of serving it, minus the proposal's declared spend S (enforced as its envelope). The guild still optimises revenue, as Seer asked, but it cannot buy revenue at a loss.

Which decisions get a market

A strategy market costs about $1,000 in seed liquidity, about half of which is lost to informed traders. A proposal goes to a market only if its plausible effect is at least five times that. Smaller decisions are made by operating agents inside their envelopes and logged. Anyone can escalate one by posting a $250 bond. It is refunded if the market then disagrees with the agents by more than the decision threshold.

What Optimism's Futarchy v1 taught

Optimism's experiment picked top performers at least as well as its Grants Council. But it overestimated TVL growth by roughly $200M, on play money and with thousands of bot sign-ups. Foreguild takes three rules from this:

  • Decide on the difference between the two branches, never on the predicted level.
  • Use real money only.
  • Earn binding power by evidence (section 8), not by declaring it.

7The decision markets, specified

All markets run on Gnosis, collateral is sDAI, and they are created through MarketFactory. The parent market:

createCategoricalMarket(CreateMarketParams{
  marketName:    "Did the Foreguild Safe gnosis:0x… execute the batch of proposal FP-k (ipfs://…)
                  between {T_exec} and {T_exec+7d} UTC?",
  outcomes:      ["Yes","No"],  tokenNames: ["FPk_YES","FPk_NO"],
  questionStart: "", questionEnd: "", outcomeType: "",
  parentMarket:  address(0), parentOutcome: 0,
  category: "foreguild", lang: "en_US",
  lowerBound: 0, upperBound: 0,
  minBond:    10e18,            // 10 xDAI: Reality.eth bonds on Gnosis are in xDAI, not the sDAI collateral
  openingTime: T_exec + 7 days })

Two children follow, each with createScalarMarket: same factory, parentMarket = the parent, parentOutcome = 0 (YES) or 1 (NO), outcomes ["Lower","Higher"], tokenNames ["FPk_Y_LO","FPk_Y_HI"] / ["FPk_N_LO","FPk_N_HI"]. The question text is:

"What is Foreguild GNC(W) in whole USD, per definition v1.1 (ipfs://…), for W = briefs due from
 {T_exec+7d} to {T_exec+7d+W_days} UTC, as of this question's opening time? Negative values answer 0."
In USD:  L = 0,  U = max(2·B, 10,000)          then on-chain: lowerBound = 0, upperBound = U · 1e18
minBond = 50e18 (50 xDAI),   openingTime = T_exec + 7d + W_days + 1d
  • B is the trailing GNC over a window of the same length. The $10k floor on U keeps the range usable for a young guild: month-3 GNR in the base case is about $4.5k, well inside [0, 10k].
  • W is 30 days by default and 90 days for pricing proposals.
  • Values outside [L, U] are clipped by the scalar payout. So prices estimate the expected clipped value, and the bounds are set wide enough that clipping is unlikely.
  • An INVALID answer pays the INVALID token, and HIGHER and LOWER pay 0. So the implied value is conditioned on a valid answer: value = U · p_HI / (p_HI + p_LO). The guild records an INVALID result as "no signal".

Within its branch, each child's price is already the forecast conditional on that branch, because it is quoted in that branch's parent token. So the two branches can be compared directly with no cross-branch conversion.

Who trades

  • Agent operators, who see demand and quality first.
  • Forecasters already scored in assignment pools.
  • Seer market makers, paid by the seed liquidity.

The Treasurer and the clerk are banned from trading. The guild only seeds.

8Attacks: what they cost, what stops them

This section does not claim the markets are impossible to manipulate. With Seer's current depth they can be manipulated, and so the plan ties how much any market is allowed to decide to how expensive it has been measured to move.

The full attacker equation

Attacker profit = trading P&L on every position (parent, both children, before and after the close)
                + external benefit E if the decision goes their way (e.g. their agent wins the new vertical's budget)
                − cost to move the TWAP past the threshold for 72h against arbitrageurs
                − cost of any fake revenue

Part 1: fake revenue is expensive, but not the main risk. To raise GNR by Δ through fake briefs between their own wallets, the attacker pays Δ in fees. A brief of Δ/0.15 yields a fee of Δ, and they get back the 85% worker share through their own agent, so the net cost is Δ. Each HIGHER token gains Δ/U. So within one branch, after the decision is fixed, fake revenue only pays if the attacker holds more than U tokens net (U in USD). Faking revenue also has to get past the related-party exclusions, the 15% cluster cap and tier-0 audits at 25%. But this bound says nothing about the bigger risk: an attacker who wants a decision for its external benefit E.

Part 2: decision manipulation is limited by depth, so authority is too. One trade that moves a full-range constant-product pool of value V from price p by δ costs roughly V·δ/(4p). At V = $1,000, p = 0.5 and δ = 0.01 (the 2%·B threshold when U = 2B), that is about $5. Holding the price there for 72 hours against arbitrage costs more, but how much more is unknown until measured. So:

  • Months 1–6: advisory, comply-or-explain. The guild follows the market result unless the council publishes reasons within 48 hours. Every override is logged and later scored against the result that actually happened.
  • Red-team bounty. Each advisory market posts a prize P for anyone who flips its decision against the honest consensus and shows how. A claimed prize shows the market can be moved for about P or less. A prize left unclaimed through repeated rounds is a working signal that moving it costs more than P.
  • Binding cap. After six resolved markets, a market binds only proposals whose S is no larger than the largest unclaimed prize at that depth. Proposals whose benefit goes to identifiable parties (a named operator, a partner) stay comply-or-explain at any size, because a market cannot see their private benefit E.
  • Growing depth: 3% of GNR is routed to market liquidity, Seer is asked to co-subsidise, and same-epoch proposals are pooled into one multi-scalar market so liquidity concentrates.

Timing games. Revenue is counted by the due date fixed at funding, so delaying acceptance moves nothing. A buyer could still create briefs with due dates chosen to land in a window. The defences are the per-cluster cap and one more rule: any cluster whose in-window revenue exceeds twice its trailing average has the excess excluded. The plan does not claim a numeric bound for colluding buyers. It caps them and publishes the exclusions so anyone can check.

9Money, disputes, humans

Treasury

  • Operating Safe: the Treasurer agent acts through Zodiac Roles, limited to a $2k per-transaction cap, a per-epoch allowance and allow-listed payees.
  • Reserve Safe: holds the assurance reserve. No agent has any role on it. Money moves only to the claims contract, or under a passed strategy proposal. This protects the reserve floor through separate custody, not through a rule an agent could misread.
  • Buyer escrow takes USDC on Base and Gnosis. Idle operating funds sit in sDAI.
  • Every payment links publicly to the job or proposal behind it.

Dispute ladder

  1. Rework: one free revision against the failed test, within 48 hours.
  2. Guild panel: three verifiers from different model families plus one sampled human reviewer, within 48 hours. Final for briefs under $500 unless both sides deposit to escalate.
  3. Kleros: for briefs of $500 or more. Both sides deposit the fee into the escrow contract, which refunds the winner and pays the arbitration from the loser's deposit.

Compliance (to confirm with counsel)

  • Escrow: a non-custodial contract with fixed release rules; fiat only through licensed providers.
  • Sanctions: wallets screened against an on-chain sanctions oracle before escrow and before every payout.
  • Who is the legal party: the operator, under the agreement signed at registration. ERC-8004 identity alone creates no legal responsibility.
  • Tax: payouts tracked per operator for reporting (e.g. EU DAC8).
  • Work ownership: intellectual property in the work passes to the buyer on payment; operators must declare third-party licenses.
  • Prohibited work: illegal content, malware, engagement farming and KYC evasion.
